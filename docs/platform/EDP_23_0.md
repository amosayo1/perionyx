# Engineering Decision Packet — Phase 23.0

**Perionyx Platform Constitution — Constitutional Architecture of the Enterprise Financial Operating System**

---

**Phase**: 23.0 — Platform Constitution
**Date**: 2026-07-24
**Status**: Complete
**Documents Produced**: 32 (1 Constitution + 3 Architecture Series + 15 Platform Specs + 5 Cross-Cutting + 8 Governance)

## 1. Decision Summary

### What Was Decided

Ratify the **Perionyx Platform Constitution** — a 32-document architectural canon that establishes the permanent engineering laws, platform boundaries, capability contracts, provider driver model, canonical financial model, and governance processes for the entire Enterprise Financial Operating System. The Constitution is the highest engineering authority in Perionyx. No code may contradict it.

### Why Now

Before implementing ERP connectors (SAP, NetSuite, Dynamics 365), banking integrations (Plaid, Stripe, Wise), payment gates (ACH, wire, SEPA), identity providers (Entra ID, Okta, SAML/OIDC), AI providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini), or additional financial domains (AR, Tax, FP&A, Fixed Assets, Investments, Financial Close, Consolidation), the permanent constitutional architecture must exist. Without it, every new integration would create its own conventions, every new domain would define its own vocabulary, and every new provider would embed its own terminology into business logic — producing an incoherent platform that cannot evolve.

### The First Principle

> **Platforms outlive products.**
> **Architecture outlives implementations.**
> **Constitutions outlive architectures.**

Every previous phase built a component. Phase 23.0 defined the laws that govern all future components.

## 2. Key Decisions

### D1: Constitutional Authority — Why a Constitution vs. Ad-Hoc Architecture Docs

**Decision**: Establish a single Platform Constitution as the highest engineering authority, with explicit hierarchy (Constitutional → Architectural → Implementation) and a formal amendment process.

**Context**: Perionyx has accumulated 22+ phases of architecture decisions documented across ADRs, EDPs, Brain lessons, and scattered documentation. By Phase 22.0B.5, there were 12 ESLint rules, 5 CI scripts, 443 passing tests, and 374 Prisma models — but no single source of engineering truth. Each new phase produced its own architectural decisions without a unifying framework. The ADR catalog (25+ ADRs) captured *what* was decided but not *what the permanent laws are*.

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Continue with ADRs only** — ADRs document individual decisions but don't establish permanent laws. They can be superseded. A constitution cannot be superseded by a single ADR.
2. **Architecture Decision Records + RFC process** — RFCs are for proposals, not for establishing permanent law. RFCs resolve to ADRs, which are implementation-level decisions.
3. **Lightweight wiki/README** — Documentation decays. The Phase 22.0B.5 lesson (Principle #17) proved that documentation alone does not prevent drift.

**Trade-offs**:
- A constitution is heavyweight — every amendment requires documented rationale, impact analysis, migration plan, and unanimous review. This slows down certain types of changes.
- A constitution without enforcement is aspirational. It required tooling investment (ESLint rules, CI gates, automated audits) to become enforceable.

**Rationale**: The Engineering Constitution's own Lesson 15 ("Build constitution first") and the Brain Constitution established that constitutions precede implementations. The Platform Constitution extends this pattern to the platform architecture. The constitutional hierarchy (Constitutional → Architectural → Implementation) ensures that no individual decision can undermine permanent laws.

**Long-term Implications**: Every future engineering decision must be traceable to a Constitutional Law. ADRs and EDPs that predate the Constitution are grandfathered but may not contradict it. Future violations are caught by the amendment process, not by ad-hoc review.

### D2: Platform Decomposition — Why 15 Platforms vs. Monolith vs. Microservices

**Decision**: Decompose Perionyx into exactly 15 Platform Capabilities, each with a stable capability contract, a bounded responsibility, and a replaceable implementation.

**Context**: Phase 18.0 ("Enterprise Architecture Consolidation") inventoried 64 modules, 402 API routes, and 33 global singletons. It found duplicates (2 workflow engines, 6 event buses, 2 currency services, 2 loggers, 2 permission registries) and dead code (13 identity files with zero consumers). The consolidation phases (18.1A, 18.1B) resolved some duplicates but couldn't prevent future ones without an architectural framework.

**The 15 Platforms**:

| # | Platform | Group | Maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | IntegrationPlatform | Integration & Connectivity | 2 |
| 2 | BankingPlatform | Integration & Connectivity | 3 |
| 3 | ERPPlatform | Integration & Connectivity | 1 |
| 4 | PaymentsPlatform | Integration & Connectivity | 1 |
| 5 | IdentityPlatform | Identity & Security | 2 |
| 6 | SecurityPlatform | Identity & Security | 2 |
| 7 | AIPlatform | Intelligence & Automation | 2 |
| 8 | WorkflowPlatform | Intelligence & Automation | 2 |
| 9 | AuditPlatform | Intelligence & Automation | 2 |
| 10 | NotificationPlatform | Communication & Documents | 2 |
| 11 | DocumentPlatform | Communication & Documents | 0 |
| 12 | ObservabilityPlatform | Infrastructure | 2 |
| 13 | SearchPlatform | Infrastructure | 2 |
| 14 | StoragePlatform | Infrastructure | 0 |
| 15 | DeveloperPlatform | Infrastructure | 0 |

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Monolith** — The current codebase is already a monolith (Next.js 16 single-process). A "platform" decomposition doesn't mean physical separation — it means logical separation through capability contracts. We kept the deployment monolith while establishing platform boundaries.
2. **Microservices** — Too early. The team is small, the product is pre-v1, and the platform boundaries are still stabilizing. Microservices would add operational complexity (service mesh, inter-service auth, distributed transactions) without proportional benefit.
3. **Fewer platforms (8-10)** — Some platforms (Identity + Security) could be merged. But Identity (authentication, SSO, MFA, provisioning) and Security (encryption, rate limiting, CSRF, dependency scanning) have different lifecycles, different failure modes, and different team ownership. Merging them creates a god-platform.
4. **More platforms (20+)** — Some sub-capabilities (e.g., "rate limiting" within Security) could be separate platforms. But this creates interface explosion without proportional benefit. 15 is the right granularity — each platform maps to a distinct operational concern.

**Trade-offs**:
- 15 platforms means 15 capability contracts to maintain, test, and version. This is more interfaces than a monolith but fewer than microservices.
- Some platforms (Document, Storage, Developer) are at maturity 0 — they exist as concepts but have no implementation. This is intentional: the Constitution defines the boundary; implementation follows.

**Rationale**: The Platform Capabilities Matrix (Phase 22.0B.5 audit) identified these 15 as the minimum viable set for an Enterprise Financial Operating System. Each maps to a distinct operational concern with independent failure modes, independent evolution, and independent replaceability.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new capability must be classified as either a new Platform (requiring Constitution amendment) or an extension of an existing Platform (requiring contract amendment). Ad-hoc module creation is prohibited.

### D3: Capability Contracts — Why Stable Interfaces vs. Direct Coupling

**Decision**: Every Platform publishes a stable, versioned capability contract. Business domains depend ONLY on these contracts, never on internal implementation details. Contracts follow semantic versioning with explicit deprecation policy.

**Context**: Before the Constitution, business logic imported Platform internals directly. AP code imported banking provider types. Workflow code imported AI provider internals. Connector adapters were referenced by name from business logic. Every provider change, every API deprecation, every internal refactor risked breaking business domains.

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Direct coupling with interfaces** — TypeScript interfaces provide some decoupling but no versioning, no deprecation policy, and no enforcement that consumers don't import implementation modules.
2. **API gateway pattern** — HTTP-level decoupling via API gateway adds network latency and complexity for in-process calls. Contracts are language-level interfaces, not HTTP endpoints.
3. **Dependency injection only** — DI containers provide runtime decoupling but don't enforce the boundary at compile time. Contracts enforce at both levels.

**Trade-offs**:
- Contracts add a translation layer — provider drivers must implement the contract interface, which adds code. But this translation layer is precisely where error translation, rate limiting, and telemetry happen.
- Versioning adds overhead — every contract change requires a semver bump, compatibility check, and deprecation timeline. But this overhead prevents breaking changes from reaching consumers.

**Rationale**: Law 3 ("Every Platform Exposes Capability Contracts") and Law 10 ("Every Platform Is Replaceable") require stable interfaces. The AP domain (Phase 21A.2) demonstrated the value: 51 commands across 7 services, each consuming contracts rather than implementations. The banking platform (126 files) demonstrated the cost of not having contracts — internal APIs changed frequently, breaking downstream consumers.

**Long-term Implications**: Consumer-driven contract testing becomes mandatory. Every PR that modifies a contract interface must verify all consumers still compile. The contract registry (planned) will provide runtime discovery and validation.

### D4: Provider Driver Model — Why Thin Adapters vs. Embedded Providers

**Decision**: Provider drivers are thin adapters that translate between Provider SDKs and Platform capability contracts. They contain authentication, retries, pagination, rate limiting, error translation, and telemetry. They contain zero business logic. They can be replaced without affecting any business domain.

**Context**: Phase 18.0 found that the `automation-studio/ai/route.ts` was a "rogue route" — it bypassed the AI provider registry and used raw `fetch()` to call Gemini directly. This violated every principle of provider isolation. The ConnectorPlatform already had adapters (Plaid, QuickBooks, SAP, NetSuite, Dynamics 365) but they weren't formalized as "drivers" with a consistent interface.

**Driver Anatomy**:
```
Authentication Layer → Request Pipeline → Response Pipeline → Retry & Resilience → Webhook Handler → Capability Discovery → Telemetry
```

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Embedded providers** — Import provider SDKs directly in business logic. Rejected: Law 1 ("Business Domains Never Import Provider SDKs").
2. **Provider SDK wrappers** — Wrap SDKs with convenience methods but allow business logic to access the wrapper. Rejected: wrappers become god-objects that mix auth, retries, pagination, and business logic.
3. **Provider-specific services** — Create `PlaidService`, `QuickBooksService`, `SAPService` with different interfaces. Rejected: each service would have a different API, making provider replacement impossible without rewriting consumers.

**Trade-offs**:
- Thin adapters add a layer between business logic and provider APIs. This layer must be maintained for each provider. But this layer is precisely where cross-cutting concerns (auth, retries, rate limiting, telemetry) are centralized.
- Provider SDK updates require driver updates. But the driver boundary contains the impact — a Plaid SDK update doesn't affect business logic.

**Rationale**: Law 4 ("Provider Drivers Are Replaceable") makes this mandatory. The existing 10 connector adapters (Plaid, QuickBooks, SAP, NetSuite, Dynamics 365, Slack, Teams, Stripe, Wise, Xero) already follow this pattern informally. The Constitution formalizes it.

**Long-term Implications**: The Provider Certification Guide (governance document) defines the certification process for new drivers. Every new provider must implement the driver interface, pass contract compliance tests, and be registered in the ConnectorPlatformRegistry before production use.

### D5: Canonical Financial Model — Why Own Terminology vs. Vendor Terminology

**Decision**: Perionyx owns its financial vocabulary. External provider terminology (Plaid accounts, QuickBooks vendors, SAP business partners, NetSuite entities) is translated at the Provider Driver boundary — never propagated into the domain model.

**Context**: The Canonical Financial Model defines 30+ entities (Company, Vendor, Customer, Invoice, Payment, Journal, JournalEntry, Account, CashPosition, CashMovement, CashForecast, Approval, Policy, AuditLog, etc.) with precise attributes, relationships, lifecycles, and invariants. Every Prisma model, every API route, every service method uses these terms. The translation table maps Plaid, QuickBooks, SAP, NetSuite, and Dynamics 365 terms to canonical terms.

**Example Translation**:
| Plaid | QuickBooks | SAP | Perionyx Canonical |
|---|---|---|---|
| `account` | `Vendor` | `BusinessPartner` | `ProcurementVendor` |
| `balance` | `Bill` | `Invoice (MM)` | `ProcurementVendorInvoice` |
| `transaction` | `BillPayment` | `AccountingDocument` | `ProcurementPaymentRecord` |

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Vendor terminology in domain model** — Use QuickBooks terms ("Vendor", "Bill", "BillPayment") throughout. Rejected: creates lock-in. Switching from QuickBooks to SAP would require renaming every entity in the codebase.
2. **Generic terminology** — Use neutral terms ("Party", "Document", "Transfer"). Rejected: too vague for financial precision. "Vendor" and "Customer" are fundamentally different entities with different lifecycles.
3. **Hybrid approach** — Use vendor terms for vendor-specific features and canonical terms for cross-cutting features. Rejected: creates confusion about which term to use when.

**Trade-offs**:
- Translation adds overhead — every driver must implement a mapping layer. But the mapping is mechanical (string mapping + field transformation) and can be automated.
- Canonical terms may not match industry conventions — "ProcurementVendorInvoice" is longer than "Bill". But precision matters more than brevity in financial systems.

**Rationale**: Law 2 ("Vendor Terminology Never Enters the Domain Model") makes this mandatory. The AP domain (Phase 21A.1) validated the approach: 25 Prisma models with `Procurement` prefix, 33 enums, all using canonical terminology. When QuickBooks is integrated, the driver translates `Bill` → `ProcurementVendorInvoice` at the boundary.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new provider driver must include a translation table mapping provider terms to canonical terms. The translation table is part of the driver's documentation and test suite.

### D6: Zero Trust — Why Verify-Everything vs. Perimeter Security

**Decision**: Every request is authenticated. Every action is authorized. Every access is logged. Trust is never assumed — it is verified. This applies to internal service-to-service calls, not just external-facing endpoints.

**Context**: Phase 17.0 (Security Audit) found 23 Critical and 58 High findings. Key gaps included: no MFA, CSRF origin bypass, SSRF on webhooks, fail-open authentication, and no body size limits. Phase 17.1 (P0 Remediation) resolved 5 Critical findings. Phase 17.2 (Enterprise Security Completion) implemented MFA, fixed password comparison, added body size limits, and hardened information disclosure. The Zero Trust model was established as the architectural default.

**Seven Security Layers**:
1. Network Security (K8s Network Policy, Ingress Rate Limiting, DNS Egress)
2. Transport Security (TLS 1.3, HSTS, X-Content-Type-Options)
3. Authentication (NextAuth, MFA TOTP, Session Versioning, Account Lockout)
4. Authorization (RBAC 64 permissions, ABAC policies, Tenant Isolation)
5. Data Security (AES-256-GCM, Key Rotation, Classification, Decimal Precision)
6. Application Security (CSRF, Input Sanitization, Rate Limiting, Body Size Limits, Dependency Scanning)
7. Audit & Compliance (Immutable Logs, Security Event Logging, Webhook Verification)

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Perimeter security** — Authenticate at the edge, trust internal calls. Rejected: a compromised service can access any data without additional checks. Law 12 requires every request to be verified.
2. **Service mesh** — Use Istio/Linkerd for mTLS and authorization between services. Rejected: too early for a single-process deployment. The Constitution's Zero Trust is enforced at the application layer, not the network layer.
3. **Opt-in security** — Require security checks only for sensitive operations. Rejected: "sensitive" is a moving target. Law 12 says every request, every action, every access.

**Trade-offs**:
- Zero Trust adds latency — every request requires authentication and authorization checks. But these checks are fast (JWT validation is ~1ms, RBAC check is ~0.5ms) and can be cached.
- Zero Trust adds complexity — every endpoint must implement auth checks. But the proxy (`src/proxy.ts`) centralizes authentication, and `requireTenantContext()` centralizes tenant isolation.

**Rationale**: Law 12 ("Zero Trust Is the Default") makes this mandatory. The security audit (Phase 16.0) and remediation (Phase 17.0-17.2) proved that perimeter security fails in practice. The 7-layer defense-in-depth model ensures that no single point of failure can compromise the system.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new endpoint, every new service, every new background job must implement Zero Trust. The SecurityPlatform provides the primitives (encryption, CSRF, rate limiting, audit logging) that make this possible without each service reimplementing security.

### D7: Financial Precision — Why Decimal(38,12) vs. Floating Point

**Decision**: All monetary calculations use `Decimal(38,12)` precision. All financial operations are idempotent. All state transitions are auditable. All approvals are non-repudiable. No exceptions.

**Context**: Phase 19.0 ("Financial Core Consolidation") found 4 Prisma Float fields storing monetary values, 100+ `formatCurrency` implementations (mostly USD-only), native number arithmetic in GL allocation/cash application/tax/statement builders, and 4 conflicting `SUPPORTED_CURRENCIES` definitions. Phase 19.1 ("Financial Foundation Implementation") created `src/lib/financial-precision.ts` with 13 helper functions and migrated 4 Float fields to `Decimal(38,12)`.

**Precision Tiers**:
| Context | Precision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| All monetary amounts | `Decimal(38,12)` | 12 decimal places for intermediate calculations |
| Exchange rates | `Decimal(20,6)` | 6 decimal places for FX rates |
| Tax rates | `Decimal(10,6)` | 6 decimal places for tax rates |
| Interest rates | `Decimal(10,6)` | 6 decimal places for interest |
| Percentages | `Decimal(10,4)` | 4 decimal places for ratios |

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Floating point (number)** — JavaScript's `number` type is IEEE 754 double-precision. `0.1 + 0.2 !== 0.3`. For financial calculations, this is unacceptable.
2. **Integer cents** — Store amounts in cents (integers). Rejected: doesn't handle currencies with 0 decimal places (JPY), 3 decimal places (KWD), or 4 decimal places (intermediate calculations).
3. **Decimal(19,4)** — Industry standard for many ERP systems. Rejected: 4 decimal places are insufficient for intermediate calculations (tax allocation, FX conversion, percentage splits). 12 decimal places prevent rounding drift in multi-step calculations.

**Trade-offs**:
- `Decimal(38,12)` is slower than native `number` for arithmetic. Prisma's Decimal type involves string serialization. But financial correctness is non-negotiable.
- 12 decimal places are more precision than most currencies need. But intermediate calculations (allocating $100 across 7 targets with different tax rates) accumulate precision that 4 decimal places would lose.

**Rationale**: Law 6 ("Financial Integrity Is Never Compromised") and Principle #3 ("Financial precision is non-negotiable") make this mandatory. The `financialRound()` function uses banker's rounding via `Intl.NumberFormat` — rounding happens at display time, not calculation time.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new monetary field in every new Prisma model must use `Decimal(38,12)`. Every new calculation must use the helpers in `src/lib/financial-precision.ts`. Native `number` arithmetic for financial values is a constitutional violation.

### D8: Tenant Isolation — Why Row-Level Security vs. Schema Isolation vs. Database Isolation

**Decision**: Every data access, every API call, every background job must be scoped to a tenant. Cross-tenant access is never permitted. Tenant context flows through every layer via `companyId` field-level isolation.

**Context**: Perionyx is a multi-tenant platform serving CFOs, Treasurers, Controllers, Finance Managers, and Auditors across multiple companies. Phase 17.0 found that CRM had missing tenant isolation (IDOR vulnerability). Phase 17.1 remediated it by adding `companyId` to all 17 CRM methods. The AP domain (Phase 21A.1) designed tenant isolation into every Prisma model from the start — all 25 models have `companyId` fields.

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Schema isolation** — Separate PostgreSQL schemas per tenant. Rejected: adds migration complexity (every migration must run N times), limits cross-tenant analytics (required for benchmarking, compliance), and doesn't work with Prisma's schema-first approach.
2. **Database isolation** — Separate databases per tenant. Rejected: maximum isolation but maximum operational complexity. Backup, restore, and migration per-tenant. Not feasible for a SaaS product with many tenants.
3. **Application-level filtering** — Filter queries by `companyId` at the service layer. Rejected: too easy to forget. A single missed filter creates an IDOR vulnerability.
4. **Row-level security (PostgreSQL RLS)** — Database-level policies that automatically filter by tenant. Partially adopted: `companyId` is the primary isolation mechanism, enforced at the repository layer via `requireTenantContext()`. RLS can be added as a defense-in-depth layer.

**Trade-offs**:
- Row-level `companyId` filtering is simpler to implement and audit than schema/database isolation. But it requires discipline — every query must include the filter.
- `requireTenantContext()` centralizes the check but doesn't prevent direct Prisma queries that bypass it. Prisma middleware can enforce this globally.

**Rationale**: Law 11 ("Tenant Isolation Is Absolute") makes this mandatory. The AP domain validated the approach: all 25 models have `companyId`, all 22 repositories filter by `companyId`, and all 51 commands receive `companyId` from `requireTenantContext()`.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new Prisma model must include `companyId`. Every new repository method must filter by `companyId`. Every new API route must call `requireTenantContext()`. Every new background job must receive `companyId` from its trigger context.

### D9: Event Architecture — Why Canonical Events vs. Vendor Webhooks

**Decision**: No vendor webhook leaves the Integration Platform directly. Every external event is normalized into canonical Perionyx events before propagating. Business domains consume only canonical events.

**Context**: The AP domain (Phase 21A.2) established an in-process typed event bus (`APDomainEventBus`) with 63 typed events across 7 categories. The ConnectorPlatform already has webhook handlers for Plaid, QuickBooks, and others. But the normalization wasn't formalized — some webhook handlers emitted provider-specific events, others emitted canonical events.

**Event Categories (AP domain example)**:
1. Vendor events (created, updated, suspended, blocked)
2. Invoice events (received, validated, matched, approved, rejected, paid)
3. Match events (initiated, completed, failed, exception)
4. Exception events (created, assigned, resolved, escalated)
5. Approval events (requested, approved, rejected, escalated)
6. Payment events (proposed, approved, processing, completed, failed)
7. Reconciliation events (initiated, matched, exception)

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Direct vendor webhooks** — Forward provider webhook payloads directly to business domains. Rejected: business domains would need to understand Plaid's webhook format, QuickBooks's webhook format, SAP's IDoc format, etc. This violates Law 2 (vendor terminology in domain model).
2. **Polling only** — No webhooks, only periodic polling. Rejected: polling introduces latency (minutes vs. seconds) and wastes resources (polling unchanged data).
3. **Event adapter pattern** — Transform provider events at the handler level, emit canonical events. This is the chosen approach. The webhook handler normalizes the payload, then emits a canonical event.

**Trade-offs**:
- Normalization adds latency — the webhook handler must parse, transform, and validate before emitting. But this latency is measured in milliseconds, not seconds.
- Normalization adds code — each webhook handler must implement a translation layer. But the translation is mechanical and can be tested independently.

**Rationale**: Law 14 ("Events Are Vendor-Neutral") makes this mandatory. The AP domain event bus demonstrated the value: 63 typed events consumed by multiple services (InvoiceService, ApprovalService, PaymentService, ReconciliationService) without any knowledge of the originating provider.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new provider webhook must be normalized before emitting. The normalized event format must match the canonical event catalog. Business domains must never import webhook-specific types.

### D10: Observability Constitution — Why Mandatory Instrumentation vs. Optional

**Decision**: Every call to an external provider, every database query, every queue operation, and every cache access must be instrumented with metrics, structured logs, and distributed traces. Unobservable dependencies are unmanageable.

**Context**: The Observability Platform (`src/server/observability/`, 13 files) provides MetricsRegistry (Counter/Gauge/Histogram), structured Pino logging, span-based distributed tracing, OpenTelemetry bridge, health checks (5 standard: cache, memory, uptime, queues, persistence), and Prometheus export. But adoption is inconsistent — some services are fully instrumented, others have no metrics.

**Eight Metric Domains**:
1. HTTP (request count, latency, error rate)
2. Database (query count, latency, connection pool)
3. Cache (hit rate, miss rate, eviction)
4. Queue (job count, processing time, dead-letter)
5. AI (token usage, cost, latency per provider)
6. Connector (sync count, latency, health)
7. Business (approval count, payment volume, reconciliation rate)
8. Infrastructure (memory, CPU, disk)

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Optional instrumentation** — Let each service decide what to instrument. Rejected: services under deadline pressure skip instrumentation. The uninstrumented service is the one that fails at 3 AM.
2. **Sampling** — Instrument only a percentage of requests. Rejected: for financial systems, every request matters. Sampling can miss the one anomalous request that indicates fraud.
3. **External APM only** — Use Datadog/New Relic for instrumentation. Rejected: external APM adds cost, vendor lock-in, and doesn't integrate with the in-process MetricsRegistry.

**Trade-offs**:
- Mandatory instrumentation adds code to every service. But the MetricsRegistry API is minimal (counter.inc(), gauge.set(), histogram.observe()) and the observability contract makes it easy.
- Full instrumentation increases memory usage (metric storage) and CPU (span creation). But the overhead is measurable and bounded.

**Rationale**: Law 5 ("Every External Dependency Is Observable") and Law 8 ("Every Platform Is Measurable") make this mandatory. The 8 metric domains ensure comprehensive coverage. The OpenTelemetry bridge enables external monitoring integration.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new service, every new contract method, every new background job must emit metrics, logs, and traces. The Observability Platform provides the primitives; consumers must use them.

### D11: Architecture Enforcement — Why Tooling vs. Documentation

**Decision**: Design rules are enforced through tooling (ESLint, CI pipelines, automated audits), not documentation alone. If a rule can be automated, it must be.

**Context**: Phase 22.0B.5 ("Enterprise Design Governance & Compliance") established 12 ESLint rules, 5 CI scripts, an auto-fixer, and VS Code integration for the EDL. The lesson was explicit: "Documentation decays. Tooling enforces." This lesson applies to the Platform Constitution as much as it applies to design tokens.

**Existing Enforcement**:
- **12 ESLint rules** for design tokens (no-hardcoded-colors, no-hardcoded-spacing, etc.)
- **5 CI scripts** (edl:audit, edl:fix, edl:report, edl:tokens, edl:compliance)
- **Auto-fixer** for safe violations (dry-run by default, --apply to write)
- **VS Code integration** for real-time diagnostics

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Documentation-only governance** — Write rules in markdown, rely on code review. Rejected: code review catches violations inconsistently. The reviewer must know the rule, remember to check it, and have time to enforce it.
2. **Manual architecture review board** — A team reviews every PR for architectural compliance. Rejected: doesn't scale, introduces bottlenecks, and is subjective.
3. **Runtime enforcement** — Throw errors at runtime when rules are violated. Rejected: runtime errors are too late — the code is already written, tested, and deployed.

**Trade-offs**:
- Tooling investment is significant — 12 ESLint rules + 5 CI scripts + auto-fixer + VS Code integration. But this investment pays for itself by preventing violations that would cost more to fix later.
- Tooling has false positives — the auto-fixer may suggest incorrect replacements. But false positives are caught during code review, and the tool can be tuned.

**Rationale**: Law 7 ("Architecture Is Governed Through Automation") makes this mandatory. Principle #17 ("Architecture Enforcement Through Tooling") extends this to all architectural decisions, not just design tokens.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new Constitutional Law must be enforceable through tooling. If a law cannot be automated, it must be reviewed manually. The goal is to make violations impossible to commit, not just detectable after the fact.

### D12: Platform Maturity Model — Why 0-4 Levels vs. Binary Ready/Not-Ready

**Decision**: Every Platform is assessed on a 5-level maturity scale (0–4): Not Started, Scaffolded, Functional, Production Ready, Optimized. Maturity is earned through evidence, not declared through intent.

**The Levels**:

| Level | Name | Key Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Not Started | No implementation. Contract not defined. |
| **1** | Scaffolded | Directory, types, interfaces. No runtime behavior. |
| **2** | Functional | Core services, happy-path logic, some tests. Not production-ready. |
| **3** | Production Ready | All contracts implemented, tested, observable, error-handled. |
| **4** | Optimized | Performance-tuned, auto-scaling, chaos-tested, SLOs tracked. |

**Current Maturity Distribution**:
- Level 0: Document, Storage, Developer, Payments (4 platforms)
- Level 1: ERP (1 platform)
- Level 2: Integration, Identity, Security, AI, Workflow, Audit, Notification, Observability, Search (9 platforms)
- Level 3: Banking (1 platform)
- Level 4: None

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Binary (ready/not-ready)** — Too coarse. A scaffolded platform is not "not ready" — it's "not started." A functional platform is not "ready" — it's "in development." Binary status loses nuance.
2. **Traffic light (red/yellow/green)** — Similar to binary but with a middle state. Still too coarse — "yellow" doesn't distinguish between scaffolded and functional.
3. **10-level scale** — Too granular. The difference between level 6 and level 7 is unclear. 5 levels provide sufficient granularity without analysis paralysis.

**Trade-offs**:
- 5 levels require evidence at each level — documentation, test results, metrics. This adds overhead. But the overhead ensures that maturity is objective, not subjective.
- Level transitions require review — moving from Level 2 to Level 3 requires demonstrating all contracts are implemented, tested, and observable. This gates production deployment.

**Rationale**: Law 8 ("Every Platform Is Measurable") and Law 9 ("Every Platform Is Testable") require objective measurement. The maturity model provides a shared vocabulary for discussing platform readiness. "Banking is at Level 3" communicates more than "Banking is ready."

**Long-term Implications**: Every platform must be reassessed quarterly. Maturity regressions (Level 3 → Level 2) trigger alerts. The goal is to reach Level 3 for all core platforms within 12 months.

### D13: Data Classification — Why 5 Levels vs. Simpler Model

**Decision**: Every piece of data has a classification level (Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted, Regulated). Handling rules (encryption, access control, retention, deletion) are determined by classification, not by convenience.

**The Five Levels**:

| Level | Description | Examples | Handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Intentionally public | Marketing content, public docs | No restrictions |
| **Internal** | Internal use only | Non-sensitive business data | Authentication required |
| **Confidential** | Business-sensitive | Financial data, employee data | Encryption + authorization |
| **Restricted** | Highly sensitive | PII, credentials, API keys | Encryption + MFA + audit |
| **Regulated** | Regulatory requirement | PCI data, healthcare data | Encryption + MFA + audit + retention + compliance |

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **3 levels (Public/Internal/Confidential)** — Too simple. PII (Restricted) and PCI data (Regulated) have different handling requirements than general confidential data.
2. **7+ levels** — Too granular. The difference between "Confidential-Internal" and "Confidential-External" is a policy decision, not a classification decision.
3. **No classification** — Handle everything as confidential. Rejected: over-encrypting public data wastes resources; under-encrypting regulated data violates compliance.

**Trade-offs**:
- 5 levels require data owners to classify their data. This adds overhead. But the overhead ensures that sensitive data is handled correctly.
- Classification must be enforced programmatically — not just documented. The SecurityPlatform provides encryption and access control primitives that respect classification.

**Rationale**: Law 13 ("Data Classification Governs Handling") makes this mandatory. Financial data (invoices, payments, balances) is Confidential. PII (email, password hash, MFA secret) is Restricted. PCI data (card numbers) is Regulated. Public data (marketing, documentation) is Public.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new Prisma model must include a classification annotation. Every new API endpoint must handle data according to its classification. The SecurityPlatform's encryption, access control, and audit primitives must respect classification levels.

### D14: Deployment Models — Why Support 5 Deployment Models vs. SaaS-Only

**Decision**: Perionyx supports 5 deployment models: Shared SaaS, Dedicated SaaS, Private Cloud, Customer Managed, and Air-Gapped. The architecture must be deployment-agnostic.

**The Models**:

| Model | Description | Target Customer |
|---|---|---|
| **Shared SaaS** | Multi-tenant on Perionyx infrastructure | SMBs, startups |
| **Dedicated SaaS** | Single-tenant on Perionyx infrastructure | Mid-market, regulated |
| **Private Cloud** | Perionyx-managed in customer's cloud | Enterprise, compliance |
| **Customer Managed** | Fully self-hosted by customer | Government, defense |
| **Air-Gapped** | No external network access | Defense, classified |

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **SaaS-only** — Maximum control, minimum operational complexity. Rejected: enterprise customers (banks, governments, defense) require on-premises deployment. SaaS-only loses these customers.
2. **SaaS + On-Premises** — Two deployment models. Rejected: "on-premises" is too broad. Customer-managed (Kubernetes) and air-gapped (no network) have fundamentally different constraints.
3. **Kubernetes-only** — All deployments use Kubernetes. Rejected: small customers don't have Kubernetes expertise. Docker Compose is sufficient for Shared SaaS.

**Trade-offs**:
- Multiple deployment models increase testing surface — every feature must work in all 5 models. But the Constitution's platform architecture (capability contracts, provider drivers) makes this feasible: the contract is the same regardless of deployment model.
- Air-gapped deployment prohibits AI providers, webhooks, and external APIs. But the Constitution's provider driver model means these capabilities degrade gracefully — the AI contract returns "not available" instead of crashing.

**Rationale**: The Enterprise Readiness Checklist (governance document) defines per-model requirements. Shared SaaS requires the least infrastructure; Air-Gapped requires the most. The architecture must not assume internet connectivity.

**Long-term Implications**: Every new feature must be tested in at least Shared SaaS and Customer Managed models. Air-Gapped compatibility is tested quarterly. Feature flags control deployment-model-specific behavior.

### D15: Constitutional Amendment Process — Why Formal Process vs. Informal

**Decision**: Amendments to the Constitution require: (1) documented rationale, (2) impact analysis on existing code, (3) migration plan for violations, (4) unanimous engineering review. Emergency amendments require post-hoc ratification within 48 hours.

**The Process**:

1. **Proposal**: Engineer writes amendment proposal with rationale, alternatives, and trade-offs
2. **Impact Analysis**: How does this affect existing platforms, contracts, and business domains?
3. **Migration Plan**: What code must change? How long will migration take? What are the risks?
4. **Review**: All engineers review and must approve unanimously
5. **Ratification**: Amendment added to Constitution with version number and date
6. **Migration**: Existing code updated to comply with new law
7. **Enforcement**: Tooling updated to catch violations

**Emergency Amendments**: If a critical security or financial integrity issue requires immediate constitutional change, the amendment can be applied immediately with post-hoc ratification within 48 hours. If ratification fails, the emergency amendment is reverted.

**Alternatives Considered**:
1. **Majority vote** — 51% approval sufficient. Rejected: a constitutional law affects every future engineer. Unanimous review ensures that no engineer is surprised by a permanent law.
2. **No process** — Any engineer can amend the Constitution at any time. Rejected: constitutions that can be changed trivially are not constitutions.
3. **External review** — Architecture board reviews amendments. Rejected: introduces external dependency and bottleneck. The engineering team owns the Constitution.

**Trade-offs**:
- Unanimous review is slow — a single objection blocks ratification. But slow ratification is better than hasty ratification of a permanent law.
- Emergency amendments bypass the process — but only temporarily. The 48-hour ratification window ensures that emergency amendments are validated retroactively.

**Rationale**: Law 15 ("The Constitution Evolves Through Process") makes this mandatory. The amendment process mirrors the Brain Constitution's governance model — permanent laws require permanent process.

**Long-term Implications**: Every proposed amendment must be documented in an EDP. The amendment history is tracked in the Constitution itself. Future engineers can trace every law to its origin.

## 3. Alternatives Rejected

### 3.1 Monolithic Architecture (Rejected)

**What**: Keep Perionyx as a single deployable unit with no internal boundaries.

**Why Rejected**: Phase 18.0 found 64 modules with no clear boundaries. Duplicates emerged (2 workflow engines, 6 event buses, 2 loggers). Without platform boundaries, every new module can import any other module, creating a dependency spaghetti that makes replacement impossible.

**What We Chose Instead**: A monolithic deployment with logical platform boundaries enforced through capability contracts. We get the operational simplicity of a monolith with the architectural discipline of platforms.

### 3.2 Microservices (Rejected)

**What**: Split Perionyx into independent services communicating over HTTP/gRPC.

**Why Rejected**: Too early. The team is small (single-digit engineers), the product is pre-v1, and the platform boundaries are still stabilizing. Microservices would add: service mesh, inter-service authentication, distributed transactions, network partitions, independent deployment pipelines, and operational overhead.

**What We Chose Instead**: A modular monolith with platform contracts that could be physically separated in the future if needed. The contracts are the seams that enable future microservice extraction.

### 3.3 Vendor-Owned Domain Model (Rejected)

**What**: Use QuickBooks terminology ("Vendor", "Bill", "BillPayment") throughout the domain model.

**Why Rejected**: Lock-in. Switching from QuickBooks to SAP would require renaming every entity. The Canonical Financial Model (30+ entities with precise attributes, relationships, lifecycles, and invariants) ensures that Perionyx owns its vocabulary.

**What We Chose Instead**: Canonical terminology with translation tables at the provider driver boundary. QuickBooks "Bill" → Perionyx "ProcurementVendorInvoice". SAP "BusinessPartner" → Perionyx "ProcurementVendor".

### 3.4 Optional Observability (Rejected)

**What**: Let each service decide what to instrument.

**Why Rejected**: Services under deadline pressure skip instrumentation. The uninstrumented service is the one that fails at 3 AM with no metrics, no logs, and no traces. Phase 7F established 8 metric domains; the Constitution makes them mandatory.

**What We Chose Instead**: Mandatory instrumentation via Law 5 ("Every External Dependency Is Observable"). The Observability Platform provides primitives; consumers must use them.

### 3.5 Documentation-Only Governance (Rejected)

**What**: Write architectural rules in markdown and rely on code review.

**Why Rejected**: Phase 22.0B.5 proved this fails. Documentation decays. Code review catches violations inconsistently. The EDL governance (12 ESLint rules, 5 CI scripts) demonstrated that tooling prevents violations that documentation merely suggests.

**What We Chose Instead**: Automated enforcement via Law 7 ("Architecture Is Governed Through Automation"). Every rule that can be automated must be automated.

## 4. Trade-offs

### 4.1 Flexibility vs. Complexity

**Chosen**: 15 platforms with capability contracts.

**Trade-off**: 15 platforms = 15 contracts to maintain, test, and version. This is more interfaces than a monolith. But each interface enables independent evolution and replaceability.

**Mitigation**: The Platform Maturity Model gates production deployment. A platform at Level 0-1 doesn't impose contract maintenance burden because it has no implementation.

### 4.2 Precision vs. Performance

**Chosen**: `Decimal(38,12)` for all monetary values.

**Trade-off**: Decimal arithmetic is slower than native `number`. Prisma's Decimal type involves string serialization. But financial correctness is non-negotiable.

**Mitigation**: `financialRound()` at display time, not calculation time. Intermediate calculations preserve full precision. The performance cost is measurable but bounded.

### 4.3 Security vs. Usability

**Chosen**: Zero Trust — every request authenticated, every action authorized, every access logged.

**Trade-off**: More auth checks = more latency. MFA = more user friction. But security incidents cost more than auth latency.

**Mitigation**: JWT validation ~1ms, RBAC check ~0.5ms. Both cached. MFA only required for sensitive operations (payment execution, configuration changes).

### 4.4 Constitution vs. Velocity

**Chosen**: Formal constitution with amendment process.

**Trade-up**: Upfront design slows initial development. Writing 32 documents before implementing new platforms feels slow.

**Trade-off**: The Constitution prevents architectural drift that would cost more to fix later. Phase 18.0 (Architecture Consolidation) spent significant effort resolving duplicates that a Constitution would have prevented.

**Mitigation**: The Constitution is not a blocker — it's a guardrail. Implementation proceeds within the constitutional framework. The amendment process handles exceptions.

## 5. Risk Analysis

### 5.1 Over-Engineering Risk

**Risk**: 15 platforms may be too many for the current team size. Each platform requires contract maintenance, testing, and observability.

**Likelihood**: Medium
**Impact**: High — over-engineering slows development and increases cognitive load.

**Mitigation**:
- The Platform Maturity Model gates investment — Level 0-1 platforms require minimal maintenance.
- The Constitution doesn't require all 15 platforms to be built simultaneously. Platforms can remain at Level 0-1 until needed.
- The capability contract pattern reduces maintenance cost — contracts are small interfaces, not large implementations.

### 5.2 Documentation Drift Risk

**Risk**: The 32 Constitution documents may become stale as the codebase evolves.

**Likelihood**: High (documentation always drifts)
**Impact**: Medium — stale documentation leads to incorrect architectural decisions.

**Mitigation**:
- Law 7 enforces architecture through tooling, not documentation. The Constitution's legal force comes from tooling, not markdown.
- The amendment process requires impact analysis on existing code, which forces documentation updates.
- The EDP catalog provides a traceable history of every decision.

### 5.3 Adoption Risk

**Risk**: Engineers may bypass the Constitution, creating modules without platform contracts, using vendor terminology in domain models, or skipping instrumentation.

**Likelihood**: Medium
**Impact**: High — violations compound, creating architectural debt.

**Mitigation**:
- ESLint rules catch design token violations automatically.
- CI pipelines gate merges on typecheck, lint, and test.
- The contract registry (planned) will enforce that business domains import only from contracts.
- Code review reinforces constitutional compliance.

### 5.4 Evolution Risk

**Risk**: The Constitution must evolve without breaking existing code. A new law may contradict grandfathered patterns.

**Likelihood**: Medium
**Impact**: High — breaking changes to the Constitution require migration of all violating code.

**Mitigation**:
- The amendment process requires a migration plan for violations.
- Grandfathering allows existing code to continue until migrated.
- The maturity model gates production deployment on compliance.
