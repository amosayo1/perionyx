## 1. Overview

### Phase Purpose

Phase 27.1 produces the **Enterprise Product Specification (EPS)** for Perionyx's AP Reference Workflow — a set of 12 companion documents that together form the complete blueprint for the AP workflow. This is a **design-only phase**. No production code is written. Every decision is traceable to customer evidence. Where evidence is insufficient, the decision is explicitly marked **[HYPOTHESIS]**.

### First Principle

> **The best enterprise software is designed around decisions, not transactions.**

A transaction (invoice captured, payment executed) is an event. A decision (approve this invoice, escalate this exception, override this match) is an act of judgement that carries financial consequence. The AP workflow is a chain of decisions held together by transactions. Every screen, every notification, every AI recommendation exists to make one decision faster, more accurate, and more trusted.

This principle inverts the traditional ERP design pattern. SAP, Oracle, and NetSuite model the transaction flow first and add decision points second. Perionyx models the decision flow first and wraps transactions around them. Evidence: Adeel Aslam (E1) spends time on "manual oversight to ensure accuracy" — oversight is decision-making, not transaction-processing.

### Scope

This packet documents **10 key engineering and product decisions** that shape the AP Reference Workflow. Each decision includes the alternatives considered, the rationale, the evidence, and the consequences.

## 2. Key Decisions

### D-01: 10 Workflow Stages (Simplified from 14)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Decision** | The AP Reference Workflow has **10 stages**, reduced from the 14-stage v1.0 design in Phase 27.0A. |
| **Rationale** | Phase 27.0A defined 14 stages for completeness. During validation, 4 stages were identified as friction points where handoff added administrative overhead without adding financial control. Specifically: Evidence Collection and Exception Detection (stages 3 and 4 in v1.0) were merged because evidence collection failures *are* exceptions — separating them forced a handoff between two system processes. Coordination and Approval (stages 6 and 7) were merged because cross-department coordination is a prerequisite for approval, not a separate stage. |
| **Alternatives** | (a) Keep 14 stages — rejected because it increases workflow complexity without corresponding control improvement. (b) Reduce to 8 stages — rejected because critical distinctions (Payment Readiness vs. Treasury Approval) would be lost. (c) 10 stages with configurable sub-stages — deferred to v2.0. |
| **Evidence** | Phase 20.0 friction analysis identified "handoff overhead" as a recurring pattern across workflows. Stage 3→4 handoff in v1.0 would have created artificial friction between two system processes. Mohamed Gamal (E5): "approval bottlenecks" — adding stages increases bottlenecks, not reduces them. |
| **Impact** | Simpler workflow model, fewer state transitions, reduced cognitive load. Exception detection naturally integrated with evidence collection. | [CONSEQUENCE] Stage 2+3 combined validation and matching may require splitting if system performance requires parallel evidence collection and matching. |
| **Validated** | Working — supported by Phase 20.0 analysis and Mohamed Gamal evidence. [HYPOTHESIS] that the simplified model does not miss any critical control points. |

### D-02: 5 State Machines (Invoice, Payment, Approval, Exception, Vendor)

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Decision** | The AP workflow is governed by **5 independent but interconnected state machines**: Invoice (12 states, 23 transitions), Approval (7 states, 12 transitions), Payment (8 states, 11 transitions), Exception (6 states, 8 transitions), Vendor (4 states, 6 transitions). Total: 37 states, 60 transitions. |
| **Rationale** | Each machine has a clear lifecycle boundary. Exception has its own machine because (a) its lifecycle is independent of invoice state (exceptions can be opened, investigated, resolved, and closed while the invoice remains in a holding state), (b) exceptions have their own SLA and escalation paths, (c) exceptions aggregate domain knowledge about process failures. Approval has its own machine because multi-level chains, delegation, counter-signature, and escalation are complex enough to warrant independent modelling. |
| **Alternatives** | (a) Single monolithic state machine (Invoice has all sub-states embedded) — rejected because it would create 37×37 = 1,369 possible transitions in a single matrix, making the machine untestable. (b) 3 machines (Invoice, Payment, Vendor) — rejected because Approval and Exception are first-class domain concerns with independent lifecycles. (c) 7 machines (add Credit Note, Batch) — rejected because Credit Note follows Invoice lifecycle closely and Batch is an execution wrapper, not a lifecycle. |
| **Evidence** | Phase 21A.0 domain architecture validated the 5-machine model during design review. Adeel Aslam (E1): "manual oversight to ensure accuracy" — exception handling is the primary oversight activity, warranting its own machine. |
| **Impact** | Clean separation of concerns. 60 well-defined transitions vs. 1,369 potential in monolithic approach. Cross-machine rules (section 7 of WORKFLOW_STATE_MACHINE.md) provide the control matrix. | [CONSEQUENCE] Cross-machine transitions require coordination (X-01 through X-12). Optimistic locking prevents race conditions. |
| **Validated** | Validated — Phase 21A.0 domain architecture established this model through 10 design decisions. |

### D-04: AI Explains But Never Decides

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Decision** | AI in the AP workflow **prepares evidence, makes recommendations, surfaces anomalies, and explains its reasoning — but never makes final decisions** about approval, payment, rejection, or exception resolution. Every decision that moves money or changes invoice state requires human authorisation (except system operations like automated evidence collection). |
| **Rationale** | Financial decisions carry legal and regulatory consequences. An AI that approves a payment creates liability questions: who is responsible if the payment is wrong? The CFO, not the AI. Additionally, customer evidence shows that trust in AI recommendations for financial decisions is not yet established. |
| **Alternatives** | (a) Full AI autonomy with human exception — rejected as premature. Legal/regulatory concerns. (b) AI suggests, human decides — accepted. This is the Perionyx Product Constitution position. (c) AI decides for low-value, human for high-value — deferred to v2.0. |
| **Evidence** | P5 (AI Explains But Never Decides). T6 (AI Forecasting Is Interesting but Untrusted, 2 sources, Working). Ayman Shawky (E3): "Need for confidence scoring on forecasts" — customers want AI to show confidence, not to decide. [CONSTITUTION] Product Constitution establishes this as inviolable for v1.0. |
| **Impact** | Every AI output is: (1) transparent — shows confidence, evidence, and reasoning; (2) actionable — user can accept, reject, or modify; (3) audited — all AI outputs logged in `AIRecommendationLog` with user action. | [CONSEQUENCE] AI cannot be the sole resolver for any workflow stage. Human-in-the-loop required for Stage 5 (Approval), Stage 4 (Exception Resolution), Stage 7 (Treasury Approval). |
| **Validated** | Validated — Product Constitution P5. Customer evidence supports. Legal/regulatory alignment. |

## 3. Alternatives Considered

### Rejected Architectures

| Alternative | Why Considered | Why Rejected | Evidence |
|-------------|----------------|--------------|----------|
| **Microservices** | Each state machine as separate service | Transactional integrity requires a shared database. 12 cross-machine rules (X-01 through X-12) would become distributed transactions. | Phase 21A.2 Unit of Work pattern. Transactional boundaries require same-database. |
| **Event Sourcing** | Full event store for invoice lifecycle | Audit trail append-only serves the same purpose. Event sourcing adds complexity (snapshotting, rebuilding, projection management) without commensurate benefit for a workflow with <20 states per entity. | Phase 21A audit chain is append-only with SHA-256. Event sourcing overkill for bounded state space. |
| **CQRS with separate read models** | Query performance optimisation | 65 API endpoints with current Prisma query patterns perform adequately at projected 500K invoice volume. Premature optimisation. | Phase 25.5 performance assessment: Prisma with proper indexes supports 500K rows. |
| **Workflow engine (Temporal/Camunda)** | External workflow orchestration | Adds operational complexity (external service, state management, recovery). Internal state machines with optimistic locking provide sufficient reliability for AP workflow. | Phase 21A.4 87 integration tests passed without external engine. |
| **Separate services per domain** | Vendor, Invoice, Payment as independent services | Cross-machine rules (X-01 through X-12) require transaction coordination. Service boundaries match aggregate boundaries, not domain boundaries. | Phase 21A.2 application services (7 services) already provide bounded context separation through code. |

### Rejected Product Decisions

| Alternative | Why Considered | Why Rejected | Evidence |
|-------------|----------------|--------------|----------|
| **14 workflow stages** | Complete granularity | 4 stages merged due to friction without control benefit. | Phase 20.0 friction analysis (handoff overhead). |
| **AI decides for low-value invoices** | Efficiency for high-volume low-value | Legal/regulatory concerns. Customer trust not established. Premature for v1.0. | P5 (AI Explains But Never Decides). T6 (AI Untrusted). |

| **Mobile-first design** | Executives are mobile-heavy | AP workflow is fundamentally desktop-based (detail review, document attachment, multi-level approval). Mobile complements; desktop leads. | Phase 27.0A persona analysis. Phase 22.0B EDL responsive guidelines. |

### Detailed Rejection Rationale: Microservices Architecture

The strongest alternative considered was a microservices decomposition with separate services per aggregate root (Invoice Service, Payment Service, Approval Service, Exception Service, Vendor Service). This was rejected for three reasons:

**Reason 1 — Cross-Machine Transactional Integrity**: 12 cross-machine rules (X-01 through X-12 in WORKFLOW_STATE_MACHINE.md) require atomic transitions across multiple aggregates. For example, X-05 (Payment confirmed → Invoice PAID) requires updating both the Payment aggregate AND the Invoice aggregate in the same transaction. With microservices, this becomes a distributed transaction requiring either saga orchestration (complex) or two-phase commit (fragile). The Phase 21A.2 Unit of Work pattern solves this with Prisma interactive transactions in a single database.

**Reason 2 — Development Velocity**: At Phase 25.5, the architecture scored 5.7/10 for platform readiness. Adding inter-service communication, service discovery, circuit breakers, and distributed tracing to a codebase still resolving 30 architectural debt items is premature. The Phase 21A architecture (modular monolith with bounded contexts) allows the team to deliver v1.0 faster and decompose later if scale demands it.

**Reason 3 — Query Complexity**: The AP workflow UI requires composite views: "Show me all invoices with their current approval level, pending exceptions, and payment status." This is a join-heavy query pattern that is trivially satisfied with a single-database query and becomes complex with microservices (API composition, CQRS, or GraphQL federation). Premature decomposition would add weeks of query infrastructure work.

**Evidence**: Phase 25.5 architecture review (5.7/10 readiness). Phase 21A.4 integration tests (87/87 passing with modular monolith). Platform Constitution Law 15: "Architecture evolves through constitution process" — microservices are a v2.0+ concern. |

## 4. Risks

### Risk Response Strategies

| Strategy | Definition | AP Workflow Application |
|----------|-----------|------------------------|
| **Avoid** | Change scope or approach to eliminate the risk | Single-currency v1.0 avoids multi-currency FX risk. AI recommends instead of decides avoids regulatory risk. |
| **Mitigate** | Reduce likelihood or impact through proactive action | Design partner programme reduces likelihood of building wrong product. Optimistic locking reduces concurrency conflict impact. |
| **Transfer** | Shift risk to a third party | Bank integration risk is shared with banking providers. AI model risk is shared with model providers (OpenAI, Anthropic). |
| **Accept** | Acknowledge risk with no proactive action | OCR accuracy variance for low-volume languages accepted at v1.0. Performance at 500K+ invoices validated after launch. |

### Risk Scoring Methodology

Each risk is scored on Likelihood (Low/Medium/High) and Impact (Low/Medium/High). The combination determines response priority:

| Likelihood × Impact | Low Impact | Medium Impact | High Impact |
|---------------------|-----------|---------------|-------------|
| **Low Likelihood** | Accept | Accept | Monitor |
| **Medium Likelihood** | Accept | Mitigate | Mitigate |
| **High Likelihood** | Monitor | Mitigate | Avoid |
